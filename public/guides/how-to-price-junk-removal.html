How to Price Junk Removal

Junk removal is priced by the space the load takes in the truck, with item minimums and surcharges on top. Here are the published rates, what moves a quote, and a worked example.

Typical prices for junk removal

These are consumer-facing figures from 2026 cost guides, not rules. They show where the market sits so you can tell whether your price is far above or below it.

Other published guides give per-yard rates: LoadUp and Dropcurb quote about $30 to $65 per cubic yard for household junk, citing HomeGuide's $1.50 per cubic foot (about $40.50 per cubic yard), and Dropcurb cites $70 to $83 per cubic yard for heavier construction debris. Treat those as secondary figures. Rates vary by region: check what competitors near you charge.

How much junk is on the job

Estimate the load in cubic yards, then convert it to a truck fraction. Dropcurb notes that 1 cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, so a pile 3 ft by 3 ft by 3 ft is about one cubic yard. Walk the whole property, including garage, attic and basement, and confirm what is staying. Most companies price by volume rather than weight, so a bulky light couch costs more than a small heavy safe, but concrete, dirt and roofing are usually charged by weight.

What moves the price

  • Volume. The space the load takes in the truck is the main driver, and Angi says a minimum of about 2 cubic yards often applies even to small hauls.
  • Type of junk. Appliances, mattresses, electronics and tires carry disposal or recycling fees. Construction debris is heavy and costs more per yard.
  • Access. Stairs, basements, long carries and tight driveways add crew time. Another guide, Jiffy Junk, adds 20 to 30% for stairs and basements; use your own labor time instead of copying that figure.
  • Dump fees. Angi notes that disposal fees are set by each municipality and are passed on to the customer.
  • Timing. Same-day or rush service can carry a premium.
  • Crew size. A whole-house cleanout needs more people than a curbside pickup.

Check the price against your costs

Add crew hours, truck cost, fuel, the dump fee and overhead, then see what margin is left. Dump fees depend on weight, so a half load of furniture and a half load of debris are not the same cost to you. See how to calculate labor cost and your overhead rate for the inputs.

Set a minimum charge

A single item still costs the drive, a two-person crew and a dump stop. Angi shows minimums of $60 to $150 for a single bulky item. Set yours from your hourly cost times the real time a small job takes, and show it as its own line.

A worked quote

One garage cleanout: about half a truckload of mixed household items, one refrigerator and a second-floor carry, using example rates (yours will differ):

Half truckload of household junk, about 7 cu yd$300.00
Refrigerator (separate item)$120.00
Second-floor carry$50.00
Total$470.00

Check it against the market: the $300 half load sits inside Angi's $200 to $400, which works out to $300 ÷ 7 = $42.86 per cubic yard, inside the $30 to $65 household range. The $120 refrigerator is inside Angi's $60 to $250 appliance range. The $50 carry fee is an example, not a published rate. These figures are examples, not recommended prices.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a flat price before seeing the load, then absorbing the extra volume.
  • Ignoring dump fees on heavy loads such as concrete, dirt or roofing.
  • Hiding appliance, mattress and stairs fees inside the load price.
  • Skipping a written estimate. Read how to write a contractor estimate for what to include.
