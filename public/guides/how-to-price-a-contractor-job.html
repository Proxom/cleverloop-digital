How to Price a Contractor Job

Most underpriced quotes have the same cause: the price is built by adding a percentage on top of cost (markup) when the owner was thinking in margin, and overhead is left out. Here is a simple four-step method that works for plumbers, electricians, painters, roofers, handymen and other home-service trades.

The short version: price = (materials + burdened labor + other job costs + overhead) ÷ (1 − target margin). The rest of this page shows how to get each number right, a full worked example, how to set a minimum charge, and what a discount really costs you.

Step 1: Add up direct costs

Direct costs are what this job alone costs you:

  • Materials, at today's supplier price, plus waste and offcuts (see the material waste calculator) and sales tax if you pay it.
  • Labor hours × your cost per hour, including your own hours if you work on the job.
  • Everything else this job needs: subcontractors, permits, equipment rental, dump fees, parking, and drive time if it is long.

Use the cost of an hour, not the wage

An hour of labor costs you more than the wage: payroll taxes, workers' comp, insurance, paid leave and retirement come on top. In the US Bureau of Labor Statistics survey for June 2026, employers in the construction industry paid an average of $36.13 per hour worked in wages and $15.84 in benefits (BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation). That is about 44 cents of benefits for every wage dollar. A small shop with fewer benefits will be lower, so work out your own figure with the labor burden calculator. At the industry ratio, a $24.50 wage costs roughly $35 an hour, the labor rate used in the example below.

Estimate hours from your history

Most jobs take longer than the first guess. Compare your last few similar jobs: if they ran 20% over the estimate, a job you think is 20 hours should be priced at 24. Add trip time, setup, cleanup and the material run, which are easy to leave out.

Step 2: Add overhead

Overhead is the cost of running the business that no single job pays for: truck, insurance, phone, software, accounting, advertising and the unpaid hours you spend quoting and invoicing. Express it as a percentage of direct costs using last year's books: for example, $42,000 of overhead ÷ $350,000 of direct costs = 12%. If you are just starting, list a year of expected overhead and divide by the direct costs you expect to bill. The overhead rate guide walks through it.

Step 3: Price from margin, not markup

Price = total cost ÷ (1 − target margin). Adding 30% on top of cost gives only a 23% margin (why). If you want 30% of the price to be profit, divide by 0.70. The markup vs margin calculator converts between the two.

Step 4: Check discount, deposit and break-even

Before you send it, know your break-even price (total cost) and what happens if the client asks for money off. A discount comes straight out of profit, which is why it hurts so much more than it looks.

Worked example

Materials$1,200.00
Labor: 24 hours × $35 (20 hours estimated + 20% from past jobs)$840.00
Equipment rental$300.00
Direct costs$2,340.00
Overhead 12%$280.80
Total cost (break-even)$2,620.80
Price at 30% margin: $2,620.80 ÷ 0.70$3,744.00
Profit$1,123.20 (42.9% markup on cost, $46.80 per labor hour)
If the client gets 10% off ($3,369.60)profit $748.80, margin 22.2%
If the client gets 30% off ($2,620.80)profit $0: you work for free

The 10% discount takes $374.40 off the price but a third of the profit. To earn the same $1,123.20 at the discounted price you would need 1.5 jobs ($1,123.20 ÷ $748.80). Try your own numbers in the free job price calculator.

Set a minimum charge for small jobs

A one-hour repair still costs you the drive, the setup and a slice of overhead. Price your smallest visit the same way as any job and never go below it:

Seven pricing mistakes that cost contractors money

  1. Adding a markup when you meant a margin (30% on cost is only 23% of the price).
  2. Pricing labor at the wage instead of the full cost of an hour.
  3. Leaving your own hours out of the job.
  4. Forgetting waste, permits, dump fees, trip time and cleanup.
  5. Guessing hours instead of checking how long similar jobs really took.
  6. Using last year's material prices on this year's quote.
  7. Giving a discount without checking what it does to profit.

Frequently asked questions

What profit margin should a contractor charge?

There is no single right number. Pick a gross margin that leaves real profit after your overhead and your own pay, then test it: price a few recent jobs at that margin and check whether last year's total would have covered every bill and paid you. Use a higher margin on small, risky or short-notice jobs.

Should I charge by the hour or a flat price?

Flat (fixed) prices usually win more residential work because the customer knows the total, and they reward you for working efficiently. Time and materials suits repairs and jobs where you cannot see the problem until you open it up. Many contractors quote flat prices and write a time-and-materials rate on the estimate for anything outside the scope.

How do I price my own labor as the owner?

Put your own hours into the job at a real wage, the same as an employee's, plus burden. Your profit is what is left after you have been paid for your time. If your own hours are free in the estimate, the business is underpricing every job.

How much should I add for overhead?

Use your own number: last year's overhead (truck, insurance, phone, software, office, unbilled time) divided by last year's direct job costs. For example, $42,000 of overhead on $350,000 of direct costs is 12%. Recalculate it every year or when a big fixed cost changes.

What is a minimum service charge and how do I set it?

It is the lowest price you will charge for any visit, so short jobs still cover drive time, setup and overhead. Price a minimum visit like any job: minimum hours times labor cost plus trip costs, add overhead, then divide by one minus your margin. In the example on this page that is $176.

What should I do when a customer says the price is too high?

Change the scope, not the margin. Offer a smaller first phase, a cheaper material or leaving out an optional item, and show the new price. A straight discount comes out of profit: 10% off a job priced at 30% margin cuts the profit by a third.
