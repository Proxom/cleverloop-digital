How to Price HVAC Services

HVAC repair is priced with a service call fee, then hourly labor or a flat rate per repair, with parts and refrigerant on top and a premium for nights and weekends. Here are the published rates, what moves a quote, and a worked example.

Typical prices for HVAC service

These are consumer-facing figures from 2026 cost guides, not rules. They show where the market sits so you can tell whether your price is far above or below it.

Hourly, flat rate, or both

  • Service call fee, then a repair price. Angi describes the usual pattern: a fee for the assessment, then either a flat fee for a specific repair with parts and labor included, or an hourly rate plus parts. Say whether the fee is credited when the customer approves the work.
  • Flat rate for predictable jobs. Housecall Pro suggests flat rates for tune-ups and quick part swaps such as capacitors, and time and materials for troubleshooting, remodels and custom ductwork.
  • Maintenance plans. Plans at $150 to $300 a year usually bundle one or two tune-ups. They turn a slow season into recurring revenue and give you the customer's next repair call.
  • After-hours premium. HomeGuide reports emergency labor of $140 to $250 per hour, and some companies add $40 to $80 per hour for nights and weekends. Write the after-hours rate on the quote before you go out.

What moves the price

  • Season. HomeAdvisor notes technicians' time is at a premium in hot summer months and some charge up to $200 per hour.
  • The failed part. A capacitor, a blower motor and a compressor are very different jobs. Quote each repair as its own option.
  • Refrigerant type and amount. Angi says a home AC needs about 2 to 4 pounds of refrigerant per ton of cooling; older R-22 costs far more than R-410A. List refrigerant by the pound, and find the leak instead of only topping up.
  • System age and warranty. A manufacturer or home warranty can cover parts, so the customer pays labor only. Ask before you quote.
  • Access. Attic and roof units take more time.
  • Location. City-level pages, such as HomeGuide's Philadelphia page at $85 to $200 per hour, run above the national range.

Check the price against your costs

Add paid labor for every technician, the van, tools, gauges, fuel, insurance, licensing and EPA certification, software, unbillable hours and overhead, then divide by the hours you can really bill. A worked quote

An AC that runs but does not cool, with a failed capacitor, using example rates (yours will differ). The diagnostic visit is $100. Labor is 1 hour at $110: 1 × $110 = $110.00. The capacitor is $60 with your markup.

Service call / diagnostic$100.00
Labor, 1 h × $110$110.00
Capacitor (part, marked up)$60.00
Total$270.00

Check it against the market: $100 sits inside the $75 to $200 and $100 to $250 service call ranges, $110 per hour is inside Housecall Pro's $75 to $150, and $270 falls inside Housecall Pro's $130 to $295 range for a capacitor replacement. If you credit the service call to approved work, show a −$100 line and the total becomes $170. These figures are examples, not recommended prices.

Common mistakes

  • Topping up refrigerant without finding the leak, then getting called back for free.
  • Not saying whether the service call fee is credited to the repair.
  • Pricing summer emergencies at off-season rates.
  • Skipping a written estimate. Read how to write a contractor estimate for what to include.
