Markup vs Margin for Contractors

Markup and margin both describe profit, but they measure it against different numbers. Mixing them up is one of the most common reasons a contractor's year-end profit is lower than planned.

Short answer: markup is profit divided by cost; margin is profit divided by price. A 30% markup is only a 23.1% margin. To hit a margin, price the job at cost ÷ (1 − margin), not cost × (1 + margin).

The two definitions

  • Markup = profit ÷ cost. It answers "how much did I add on top of what the job cost me?"
  • Margin = profit ÷ price. It answers "how much of what the customer paid did I keep?"

Both use the same dollars of profit. Only the number you divide by changes, and the price is always bigger than the cost, so the margin is always the smaller percentage.

Example: the same job, two percentages

Job cost (materials, labor, overhead)$1,000.00
Add a 30% markup: $1,000 × 1.30$1,300.00
Profit$300.00
Margin: $300 ÷ $1,30023.1%, not 30%
Price for a true 30% margin: $1,000 ÷ 0.70$1,428.57 (profit $428.57)

On this one job, thinking "30%" but applying it as markup leaves $128.57 on the table.

What the mix-up costs over a year

Take a contractor whose jobs cost $500,000 in a year (materials, labor and overhead) and who wants a 30% margin.

MethodRevenue / profit
Marks every job up 30%: $500,000 × 1.30$650,000 / $150,000 (23.1%)
Prices every job at cost ÷ 0.70$714,285.71 / $214,285.71 (30%)
Profit missed by using markup$64,285.71

Same crews, same jobs, same hours. The only difference is which number the 30% was divided by. (This assumes customers accept either price; a higher price can lose some bids, which is why you should check your win rate after changing your pricing.)

Different markups on materials and labor

Many contractors put a smaller markup on materials (customers can look up the price) and a bigger one on labor. That is fine, but check the margin on the whole job, because that is what pays the bills.

LineCost → price
Materials, 20% markup$2,000 → $2,400
Labor (burdened), 50% markup$3,000 → $4,500
Job total$5,000 → $6,900
Profit $1,900: blended markup ($1,900 ÷ $5,000)38.0%
Blended margin ($1,900 ÷ $6,900)27.5%

The more of a job that is materials, the lower the blended margin gets. A materials-heavy job priced with the same split percentages can fall well below your target, so look at the total, not the line items.

"10 and 10": overhead and profit as markup

You will often hear "10 and 10": 10% for overhead plus 10% for profit, added to the job cost. Written as a markup it is 20%. As a share of the price:

Job cost$1,000.00
Plus 10% overhead and 10% profit$1,200.00
Overhead + profit as a share of price ($200 ÷ $1,200)16.7%
Profit alone as a share of price ($100 ÷ $1,200)8.3%

If your real overhead is higher than 10% of your costs, "10 and 10" quietly eats the profit part. Work out your own number with the contractor overhead rate guide.

Gross margin vs net margin

If overhead is not in the job cost you start from, the margin you calculate is a gross margin: it still has to pay for the truck, insurance, phone, software and office. Example: direct cost $1,000, priced at $1,428.57 (30% gross margin, $428.57 gross profit). With overhead of 12% of direct cost ($120), the net profit is $308.57, a net margin of 21.6%. Decide which one your target means, and write it down.

What discounts do to margin

A discount comes straight out of profit. On a job that costs $1,000 and is priced at $1,428.57 (30% margin), 10% off brings the price to $1,285.71 and the profit to $285.71: a third of the profit is gone. On a job marked up only 20% ($1,200), the same 10% off brings the price to $1,080 and leaves $80 of profit: a 7.4% margin on a job you thought of as "20%". If you must move on price, change the scope instead.

Spreadsheet formulas

With cost in cell B1 and price in B2 (Excel or Google Sheets):

  • Margin: =(B2-B1)/B2
  • Markup: =(B2-B1)/B1
  • Price from a target margin in B3: =B1/(1-B3)
  • Price from a markup in B3: =B1*(1+B3)

Format the results as percentages. Or skip the spreadsheet and use the free markup vs margin calculator.

Five mistakes to avoid

  1. Using your margin target as a markup. The most common one, and it costs about a quarter of the planned profit at 30%.
  2. Leaving out burdened labor. Wages are not the labor cost; add payroll taxes, insurance and benefits (see the labor burden calculator) and how to calculate labor cost.
  3. Checking line items, not the job. A low materials markup on a materials-heavy job pulls the whole job down.
  4. Not knowing if a target is gross or net. A 30% gross margin with 12% overhead is not a 30% profit.
  5. Discounting the price instead of the scope. Every dollar off is a dollar of profit.

Which one should you use?

Set your goal as a margin, because overhead and profit targets are usually stated as a share of revenue. Then price every job with: price = total cost ÷ (1 − target margin). Make sure "total cost" includes overhead and your true labor cost, not just wages. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is profit as a percentage of cost. Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price. The same job always has a higher markup than margin, because the price is always bigger than the cost.

What margin does a 30% markup give?

About 23.1%. A $1,000 cost marked up 30% sells for $1,300; the $300 profit is 23.1% of $1,300. For a true 30% margin you would charge $1,428.57.

Is a 50% markup the same as a 50% margin?

No. A 50% markup is a 33.3% margin: $1,000 of cost sells for $1,500 and the $500 profit is one third of the price. A 50% margin needs a 100% markup, which means doubling the cost.

How do I price a job from a target margin?

Price = total cost divided by (1 minus target margin). For a 30% margin on $1,000 of cost: $1,000 / 0.70 = $1,428.57. Make sure the cost includes overhead and burdened labor.

What does 10 and 10 overhead and profit mean?

It means adding 10% for overhead and 10% for profit on top of the job cost, a 20% markup in total. As a share of the price that is only a 16.7% margin, and the profit part is about 8.3% of the price.

How do I calculate markup and margin in Excel or Google Sheets?

With cost in B1 and price in B2: margin =(B2-B1)/B2 and markup =(B2-B1)/B1. To get a price from a target margin in B3: =B1/(1-B3). Format the results as percentages.
