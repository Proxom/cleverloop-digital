How to Write a Contractor Estimate

A good estimate wins the job and protects you after it. Here is the order to write it in, what to put on it, the deposit and cancellation rules that can apply, a worked example and the follow-up messages that turn estimates into jobs.

Estimate vs quote vs bid

DocumentWhat it meansUse it when
EstimateYour best calculation; the final price can change if the scope or conditions changeMost residential repair and remodel work
QuoteA fixed price for a clearly defined scopeThe scope is small and fully known
BidA formal offer competing against others, usually against scope documents the customer suppliesLarger, commercial or public jobs

Words differ by trade and state, so write on the document which one it is and what can change the price. Check your state's licensing and contract rules for any required wording (see rules that can apply below).

Write it in this order

  1. Measure and note the conditions. Take measurements and photos at the visit and write down anything that could change the price: access, old wiring, rot, slopes, parking. These notes become your exclusions and assumptions later.
  2. Write the scope in plain words. One short paragraph a homeowner can read aloud: what you will do, where, and with which materials. "Replace 3 damaged fence sections (8 ft, cedar dog-ear) on the north side; reuse the gate" beats "fence repair".
  3. Price each line from cost, then add your margin. Labor hours × your loaded hourly cost, materials with waste, disposal, permits. Add overhead and profit with a deliberate markup, not a guess. The job price calculator does this in one screen; markup vs margin explains why 25% markup is only 20% margin.
  4. List exclusions and assumptions. What is not in the price and what you assumed. This is the step most contractors skip and the one that prevents most arguments.
  5. Set the deposit and payment schedule. Tie each payment to a stage of the work, and check your state's limits first (California caps a home improvement down payment at $1,000 or 10%, whichever is less).
  6. Add the valid-until date and terms. Usually 14 to 30 days. Add how changes are handled: in writing, as a change order, before the extra work starts.
  7. Send it fast, then follow up. Send while the visit is fresh in the customer's mind, ideally the same or next day, and follow up on a schedule (below) instead of waiting.

What to put on a contractor estimate

  1. Your details. Business name, phone, e-mail, and license number if your state requires one on documents.
  2. Customer and job address. The place the work happens, not only the billing address.
  3. Estimate number and date. Numbers make later invoices and change orders easy to match.
  4. Scope of work. One short paragraph in plain words: what you will do and where.
  5. Itemized labor and materials. Lines with quantity, unit price and amount. Itemizing shows the customer what they pay for and makes it easy to adjust one line.
  6. Exclusions. What is not included (permits, disposal, painting after drywall, hidden damage). This is where most disputes are prevented.
  7. Tax, total and deposit. Show tax if it applies and the deposit you ask for to book the job.
  8. Payment schedule and dates. When each payment is due and roughly when work starts and finishes. California requires a written payment schedule and a completion date on home improvement contracts.
  9. Valid until. A date after which the price needs to be confirmed. 14 to 30 days is common.
  10. Acceptance. A line for the customer's name, signature and date, or written approval by e-mail. If you sell at the customer's home, add the right-to-cancel notice where it applies.

A worked example

A fence repair of three sections:

Labor: 6 hours × $65$390.00
Materials: 3 panels × $48, 6 posts × $14$228.00
Haul away old panels$60.00
Subtotal$678.00
Tax on materials: 8% × $228$18.24
Total$696.24
Deposit to book (30%), where your state allows it$208.87
Same job in California: lesser of $1,000 or 10%$69.62

The numbers are illustrative; tax rules for labor and materials vary by state. The same total can need a very different deposit depending on where the job is, which is why the deposit line is worth checking on every estimate.

The words around the numbers matter as much as the numbers. For the same job:

Scope: Remove and replace 3 damaged fence sections (8 ft wide, 6 ft high, cedar dog-ear pickets) on the north side of the back yard, including 6 new pressure-treated posts set in concrete. Reuse the existing gate and hardware. Haul away old panels.

Not included: permits or HOA approval, staining or sealing, repairs to sections other than the three listed, moving sprinkler lines or buried utilities found when digging, survey or property-line confirmation.

Assumptions: clear access to the fence line; utilities marked (811) before digging; no rock or old concrete footings in the post holes. Hidden conditions are quoted as a written change order before any extra work.

Valid until: 30 days from the estimate date. Payment: deposit to book, balance on completion.

Rules that can apply to your estimate

An estimate often becomes the contract once the customer signs it, so contract rules start to matter. Three examples worth knowing:

RuleWhat it says
California down payment capOn a home improvement or swimming pool contract, the down payment may not exceed $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is less, with no exception for special-order materials. Later payments may not exceed the value of work done or materials delivered. Source: Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 7159.5; CSLB
California written contractA written contract is required for home improvement projects over $500, and price or scope changes must be made with a written, signed change order. Source: CSLB
FTC Cooling-Off Rule (all states)When a consumer agrees to buy at their home (including after inviting you over) and the price is $25 or more, they can cancel within three business days, and you must give a written cancellation notice and tell them about it orally. It does not cover sales made entirely online, by mail or by phone. Source: 16 CFR Part 429; FTC

Many states add their own rules (deposit limits, required license numbers, longer cancellation periods for older homeowners, lien notices). Check with your state's contractor licensing board before you rely on a template. This is general information, not legal advice.

Mistakes that lose jobs or money

  • A one-line scope. "Repair fence — $700" invites the customer to assume everything is included.
  • No exclusions. Without them, hidden damage becomes your cost or an argument.
  • No expiry date. A customer who calls back in four months expects the old price at today's material costs.
  • Guessing the markup. Adding "about 20%" to cost without counting overhead is how busy contractors end up with no profit. Work out your overhead rate once and use it on every job.
  • A deposit your state does not allow. Check before you print it; in California 30% is not allowed on home improvement work.
  • Sending it late and never following up. Many estimates are not rejected, just forgotten.
  • Retyping it as an invoice. Retyping invites mistakes. Turn the accepted estimate into the invoice with the same lines and numbers.

Follow up on an estimate

WhenWhat to send
Same or next dayThe estimate itself, with one line on what happens next
2 to 3 days laterA short check-in: any questions, anything to change?
About a week before it expiresA reminder of the valid-until date and your next open start date
After it expiresOffer to re-confirm the price if they still want the work; then stop

Two messages you can copy and adapt:

Check-in (day 2 or 3): Hi Dana, just checking you received estimate #1042 for the fence repair ($696.24). Happy to walk through any line or adjust the scope. If it looks right, reply "approved" and I will send the deposit details and a start date.

Before it expires: Hi Dana, a quick note that estimate #1042 is valid until June 14. My next open start date is June 20. If you would like to keep that slot, reply to this message and I will book you in.

Keep it short, name the estimate number and amount, and give one clear next step. Do not chase more than three or four times.
