How to Price Electrical Work

Electrical work is priced with a service call fee, then hourly labor or a flat rate per job, with parts and permits on top and a premium for emergencies. Here are the published rates, what moves a quote, and a worked example.

Typical prices for electrical work

These are consumer-facing figures from 2026 cost guides, not rules. They show where the market sits so you can tell whether your price is far above or below it.

The sources overlap but do not agree: some include the service call and parts and some do not, and city pages run above the national range. Compare several sources and your local competitors before you set a rate.

Hourly, flat rate, or both

  • Service call fee, then the job. Charge a trip fee that covers travel and assessment, then price the work. Say whether the fee is credited when the customer approves the job.
  • Flat rate for repeatable jobs. Housecall Pro suggests flat rates for outlet installs, ceiling fan replacement, fixture installs and basic breaker work, and custom bids for panel upgrades, remodels and rewiring.
  • Emergency premium. Angi says emergency electrical work often costs double the standard rate. Write the after-hours rate on the quote before you go out.
  • Permits and inspection. Many cities require a permit for new circuits, panels and rewiring. Pass the permit cost through as its own line.

What moves the price

  • Distance from the panel. A longer wire run costs more labor and material, and a full panel can turn an outlet into a panel or subpanel job.
  • Access. Finished walls, attics and crawl spaces take longer to fish wire through.
  • Who supplies the fixture. Most fan and fixture prices assume the customer buys the fixture.
  • Licence level. A journeyperson can handle standard installs; panel and service work needs a master or licensed supervision where your state requires it.
  • Location. Large cities run well above rural rates.

Check the price against your costs

Add paid labor for every electrician, the van, tools, test equipment, fuel, insurance, licensing, software, unbillable hours and overhead, then divide by the hours you can really bill. See how to calculate labor cost and your overhead rate, and use the hourly rate calculator to find your floor.

A worked quote

Replacing a failed outlet in a kitchen with a GFCI outlet, using example rates (yours will differ). The trip fee is $100. Labor is 1.5 hours at $90: 1.5 × $90 = $135.00. The GFCI outlet and cover plate are $25 with your markup.

Service call / trip fee$100.00
Labor, 1.5 h × $90$135.00
GFCI outlet and plate (marked up)$25.00
Total$260.00

Check it against the market: $100 sits inside the $100 to $200 service call range, $90 per hour is inside the $50 to $130 range, and $260 is inside Angi's $130 to $300 range for a GFCI outlet. If you credit the trip fee to the job, show a −$100 line and the total becomes $160. These figures are examples, not recommended prices.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the permit or inspection fee on a new circuit.
  • Not saying whether the trip fee is credited to the job.
  • Quoting a panel or rewire job at a flat price before seeing the panel and access.
  • Skipping a written estimate. Read how to write a contractor estimate for what to include.
